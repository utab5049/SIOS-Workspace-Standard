# ARS-001

## SIOS Reference Scenario

**Document ID:** ARS-001
**Version:** 0.1.0-draft.1
**Status:** Draft
**Classification:** Reference Scenario (Non-Normative — this document is a story, not a specification)
**Audience:** Teams building retrieval-augmented or knowledge-grounded AI systems, evaluating whether SIOS's methodology is worth adopting
**Read time:** ~15 minutes

---

## What This Document Is (and Isn't)

This is not a specification. It doesn't define anything new, and it isn't normative — nothing here uses SHALL, SHOULD, or MAY. It's a true story about the one time, so far, that SIOS's entire research pipeline was run for real, start to finish, on a real question. If you want to understand what SIOS actually *does* — not what it says it does — read this first.

---

## The Setup

SIOS's constitution makes a specific, checkable promise: every normative requirement should be traceable to its origin (CP-005, "Traceability"), and no artifact should exist outside the project's own registry (MAI-001's founding rule). Like most such promises, it had never actually been tested. So one was run: take SIOS's research pipeline — the one specified across a dozen documents but never yet executed — and point it at a real question. The question chosen was almost boring on purpose: *does SIOS actually follow its own rule about tracking what it depends on?*

---

## Stage 1 — Source

The pipeline starts by finding and registering real sources. In this run, that meant real academic papers — a 1993 paper that coined the standard definition of "ontology," a 2023 paper by a well-known meta-science researcher critiquing how systematic reviews are actually conducted, and, eventually, two short technical documents: **RFC 2119** and **RFC 8174**, the IETF standards that define what words like "SHALL" and "SHOULD" are supposed to mean in a specification.

Those two weren't picked at random. They were picked because SIOS's own specification language document, CSL-001, already cites both of them by name, in its list of adopted external references — the same list that gives SIOS's entire normative vocabulary (SHALL/SHOULD/MAY) its meaning.

## Stage 2 — Observation

Before interpreting anything, the pipeline first extracts plain, uninterpreted statements from each source — what the source actually says, not what it implies. From RFC 2119: a definition of ten requirement-level keywords. From RFC 8174: a clarification that only the *uppercase* versions of those words carry that special meaning — because the original 1997 document had caused real confusion by saying its keywords "are often capitalized," which left lowercase usage ambiguous.

At this stage, nothing has been concluded yet. These are just two facts, sitting side by side.

## Stage 3 — Evidence

This is where interpretation happens — carefully, and only here. The two observations were compared against something outside the sources themselves: SIOS's own registry, MAI-001. The result was a specific, checkable claim: **CSL-001 has depended on RFC 2119 and RFC 8174 since the day SIOS's Foundation Baseline was frozen — and neither has ever been registered anywhere in MAI-001.**

A small, good-news detail came with it: CSL-001's own keyword table is written entirely in uppercase, so SIOS's actual practice already happens to follow RFC 8174's clarification. The gap wasn't that SIOS was doing something wrong. It was that SIOS didn't have a record proving it was doing something right.

## Stage 4 — Review

The claim didn't survive its first check unchanged. A second, deliberately skeptical pass — reading the same material with the explicit goal of finding what the first pass missed, rather than confirming it — turned up a real problem with the *proposed fix*, not the finding itself: the original plan was to patch this into ERS-001, the Evidence Record schema. But RFC 2119 and RFC 8174 aren't really *evidence* in the sense ERS-001 is built for — they're normative dependencies, a different kind of thing entirely. The fix got redirected to where it actually belonged: AERA-001D, the document that already owns SIOS's dependency rules.

This is worth sitting with for a second: the review process didn't just check "is this true," it caught "is this filed in the right place" — a subtler and, in practice, more common kind of mistake.

## Stage 5 — Framework

The finding didn't just sit as an isolated fact. It fed into a small, honest first draft of a Framework document — the kind of artifact SIOS's 03-Framework domain is supposed to eventually be full of, but wasn't yet. That draft explicitly said, in writing, which parts of its subject it had real evidence for and which parts it didn't — no filling in gaps with plausible-sounding prose.

## Stage 6 — Governance

The proposed fix — a new registration category in AERA-001D, called an "External Normative Reference," with a two-letter prefix (`ENR`) and one clear rule attached to it — was written up as an actual, ready-to-vote-on change. Not a suggestion. A redline. Classified, per SIOS's own change-management rules, as a Structural change: real enough to need real review, small enough not to need a full constitutional rewrite. Explicitly written to apply going forward, not retroactively — an already-Frozen document doesn't get blamed for a rule that didn't exist yet when it was written.

## Stage 7 — Remediation (the only step not yet done)

Once that rule is formally accepted, closing this whole story takes about five minutes: register RFC 2119 and RFC 8174 as `ENR-000001` and `ENR-000002`. That's it. That's the entire fix for a gap that had existed, unnoticed, since SIOS's constitution was first frozen.

## Why This Is the Right First Story

If you build systems that ground AI outputs in retrieved knowledge, the question you actually care about isn't "does this framework have a lot of specifications." It's: **when this system doesn't know something, or got something wrong, does it find out — and does it tell you clearly, with a fix, not an excuse?**

This story is one small, complete answer to that question, and it's a true one. Nothing here was staged. The mistake was real (an unregistered dependency, sitting quietly in a Frozen constitutional document since day one). The self-correction was real (a review pass that caught its own first fix pointing at the wrong document). The fix is real, written, and waiting on one governance vote.
